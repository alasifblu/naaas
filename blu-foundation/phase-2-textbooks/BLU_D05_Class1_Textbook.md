**Virtue: Kindness | দয়া [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]**

**Learning Objective:** By the end of this chapter, students will use "please" and "thank you" correctly in at least 3 daily situations (asking for something, receiving food, receiving help).

---

## The Story: Rafi's Magic Words
### রাফির জাদুর কথা [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

One morning, Rafi (রাফি) arrived at school with his favourite red pencil — but he had forgotten his ruler at home. He sat down next to his friend Mitu (মিতু), who had a brand-new yellow ruler on her desk.

Rafi looked at the ruler. He needed it right now. Without thinking, he reached across and grabbed it from Mitu's desk.

"Hey!" said Mitu. Her voice was quiet, but her eyes looked sad. She looked down at her empty desk and said nothing. But she didn't feel like smiling anymore.

Their teacher, Apa (আপা), had seen what happened. She came over softly and knelt beside Rafi.

"Rafi," she said gently, "how do we ask for something we need?"

Rafi felt his face go warm. He knew the answer. He looked at Mitu.

"Mitu," he said, "please may I borrow your ruler? I forgot mine at home."

Mitu's whole face changed. The little sad line went away and a big smile came instead.

"Of course!" she said, and she passed the ruler to him.

When Rafi was finished, he put the ruler back carefully on Mitu's desk.

"Thank you, Mitu," he said. "You are a very kind friend."

Mitu beamed. Apa beamed. And Rafi felt something warm and happy in his chest — the feeling that comes when you use your magic words.

# Chapter 2: Saying Hello — Greetings for Everyone
**Virtue: Respect | সম্মান [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]**

**Learning Objective:** By the end of this chapter, students will use appropriate greetings with elders, peers, and in different daily settings (home, school, mosque/community).

---

## The Story: Mitu Learns to Say Hello
### মিতু সালাম শেখে [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

One holiday, Mitu (মিতু) went with her mother to her grandmother's house in the village. Mitu was shy. When she walked through the door, she looked at her feet and said nothing. The aunties and uncles in the sitting room all smiled at her, but Mitu just hid behind her mother's sari.

Her grandmother — Nanu (নানু) — took Mitu gently by the hand and led her to the garden.

"Are you afraid?" Nanu asked.

"A little," Mitu said. "I don't know what to say."

Nanu smiled and sat down beside her. "A greeting is a gift," she said. "When you say *Assalamu Alaikum* — 'peace be upon you' — you are giving every person a gift of peace. Nobody is afraid to give a gift."

Mitu practiced the words softly. *Assalamu Alaikum. Assalamu Alaikum.*

Nanu also showed her how to say *Adab* (আদাব) — placing both hands together and bowing her head slightly — for elders of all faiths. "It means: I respect you," Nanu explained.

When they went back inside, Mitu stood at the door of the sitting room. She took a deep breath.

"Assalamu Alaikum," she said clearly, and she pressed her hands together in *Adab* for Dadu, the eldest uncle.

The whole room lit up. Everyone answered her greeting warmly. Mitu felt like a tiny sun had turned on inside her chest.

The next morning, she ran to the gate to greet every single person who arrived — neighbours, the vegetable seller, the little cousins — and each greeting felt better than the one before.

# Chapter 3: Listening is Caring
**Virtue: Respect | সম্মান [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]**

**Learning Objective:** By the end of this chapter, students will sit still, make eye contact, and wait without interrupting when someone else is speaking.

---

## The Story: Rafi Learns to Listen
### রাফি শুনতে শেখে [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

One day, Apa (আপা) was telling the class a story about a clever rabbit and a slow tortoise. It was a very exciting story. But Rafi (রাফি) kept leaning over to his friend and whispering, "Do you know this story? I know how it ends! The tortoise wins!"

Apa paused. She looked at Rafi. She didn't shout. But the other children turned to look too, and Rafi felt uncomfortable.

After school, Rafi's grandfather — Dadu (দাদু) — was waiting at the gate. As they walked home, Rafi told him what happened.

Dadu listened to every single word. He didn't say anything until Rafi had completely finished.

"Did I interrupt you?" Dadu asked.

"No," said Rafi.

"How did that feel — knowing I was listening to every word?"

Rafi thought about it. "Good. Like what I was saying mattered."

"Exactly," said Dadu. "That is what you gave Apa the power to give the whole class. But when you whispered, you took that feeling away — not just from Apa, but from all your friends."

Rafi was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "Dadu, I want to be a good listener."

"Then I will teach you," said Dadu. "Eyes forward. Mouth closed. Wait for your turn. And when you listen well — your turn will come."

The next day, Apa began another story. Rafi sat straight. Eyes forward. Mouth quiet. He listened to every word.

When Apa asked at the end, "Who can tell me what the tortoise did?" — Rafi's hand shot up, and he knew every detail. Apa beamed.

"Rafi, will you lead today's activity?" she asked.

He walked to the front of the class, tall and proud.

## Teacher Notes (Brief)

- Introduce the **"talking stick"**: a small decorated stick (or ruler, or rolled-up paper) that children pass around the circle. Only the child holding the talking stick may speak. Everyone else listens. This is a powerful, concrete tool for 6–7 year olds who need a physical cue.
- Model active listening visibly: when a child speaks to you, make exaggerated eye contact, nod, and wait for them to fully finish before responding. Name it afterwards: "Did you see what I did? I listened with my whole body."
- The reflection question ("Have you ever felt ignored?") may surface genuine emotions. Allow children to share without pressure.
- **See BLU_D10 Chapter 3 for:** full facilitation script, talking stick activity guide, circle time structure, and assessment rubric.

## The Story: Karim Finds His Shine
### করিম তার ঝলক খোঁজে পায় [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Karim (করিম) always rushed in the morning. He woke up late, skipped washing his face, pulled on yesterday's uniform, and ran to school without eating properly. By the time he arrived, his hands had mud on them from the path, his hair was sticking up in every direction, and his collar was crumpled.

The other children moved their chairs slightly when Karim sat down. Nobody said anything mean, but Karim could feel something was not right. He sat alone, feeling small.

At breaktime, Mitu (মিতু) came and sat near him. She was kind, and she didn't move away.

"Karim," she said gently, "can I ask you something? Did you wash your hands today?"

Karim looked at his hands. There was dirt under his nails.

That afternoon, Karim told Dadu (দাদু) what had happened. Dadu listened carefully.

"Do you know," said Dadu, "that in our faith, cleanliness is half of faith? The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught us: *'Cleanliness is half of faith.'* (Al-Tirmidhi) A clean body and a tidy appearance shows respect for yourself, for others, and for Allah."

Then Dadu helped Karim make a morning plan: wake up → wash face and hands → brush teeth → comb hair → wear a clean uniform → say *Bismillah* and go to school.

The next morning, Karim followed every step. When he arrived at school, Mitu looked up and smiled.

"Karim! You look great today!"

Karim stood taller. He looked at his clean hands. He felt different — lighter, prouder, happier. And this time, no one moved their chair away.

## Teacher Notes (Brief)

- If possible, bring a small bottle of hand soap and a basin or bucket of water to class for a live handwashing demonstration. Show the correct technique: both hands, 20 seconds, fingertips and thumbs.
- Frame cleanliness explicitly within both Islamic values (*Taharah* — purity) and general health. For mixed-faith classrooms, the health framing is universal; the religious framing can be offered as additional meaning for Muslim students.
- The story is specifically written so that the child who needs to improve (Karim) is not shamed by peers but helped by a kind friend. Maintain this tone — the lesson is never about mocking anyone who lacks hygiene habits; often those habits depend on home circumstances outside the child's control.
- **See BLU_D10 Chapter 4 for:** handwashing demonstration guide, morning routine poster template, home-school communication tip for parents who may need support.

## The Story: Mitu's Happiest Breaktime
### মিতুর সবচেয়ে আনন্দের টিফিনের বিরতি [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

One Thursday, Mitu (মিতু) came to school with a big paper bag. Inside was a whole packet of chanachur (চানাচুর) — the spicy, crispy snack that all children love. Her mother had packed extra as a surprise.

At breaktime, Mitu sat under the banyan tree and opened her bag. The smell drifted out, and soon four of her classmates were looking her way — not asking, just noticing.

Mitu looked at the big bag and then at her friends. She thought: *I could eat it all. It's mine. Nobody would blame me.*

Then she remembered something Dadu (দাদু) had told her last week. She had asked him, "Dadu, what is the happiest feeling in the world?"

He had smiled and said: "The smile on your friend's face when you share something with them. That is a happiness you cannot eat or drink or buy. You can only find it by giving."

Mitu looked at the bag, and then at her friends, and then she made up her mind.

"Come!" she said. "Come and sit with me."

She opened the bag wide and passed it around. Each friend took a small handful. The bag went around the circle and came back to Mitu — still half full.

"Thank you, Mitu!" said Rafi. "You are the best friend."

"Thank you, Mitu!" said the others.

Mitu ate her chanachur slowly, looking at her friends' happy faces. The snack tasted just as good as always. But something else tasted even better: the warm, glowing feeling that filled her from head to toe.

It was the happiest breaktime she had ever had.

## Teacher Notes (Brief)

- Facilitate a **class sharing circle** this week: ask each child to bring one item from home that they are willing to share (a book, a small toy, a drawing). In the circle, each child passes their item to the child on their right. Discuss the feelings together afterwards.
- If children do not have items to bring, the circle works equally well with classroom resources: crayons, stickers, or cards made in class.
- Sharing can be a genuinely difficult concept for young children, particularly those from households with scarcity. Never pressure a child to share something if they are genuinely distressed. The discussion of feelings is as important as the act itself.
- **See BLU_D10 Chapter 5 for:** full lesson script, sharing circle facilitation guide, assessment tool, and extension activities for children who are ready.
