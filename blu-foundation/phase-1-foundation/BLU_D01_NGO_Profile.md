## 1. Executive Summary

Blu Foundation is a Bangladesh-based non-governmental organisation (NGO) established to rebuild a culture of manners, etiquette, and respectful behaviour starting with the nation's youngest citizens. Recognising that primary school children in Classes 1–5 occupy the most formative window for character development, Blu Foundation works directly within schools and communities to fill a critical and well-documented gap: Bangladesh's national curriculum nominally includes values education but delivers it nowhere in practice [SOURCE_BD_07]. The result is a generation of children growing up without structured, culturally grounded guidance on how to treat one another with dignity — a deficit that research links directly to bullying, social deviance, and juvenile delinquency [SOURCE_BD_12].

Blu Foundation addresses this gap through a multi-channel programme model. At its core, the organisation develops dedicated, peer-reviewed textbooks on manners and social etiquette adapted specifically to Bangladeshi cultural and religious context. These materials are supported by structured teacher training programmes that equip approximately 400,000 primary school educators nationally with the pedagogical tools to deliver character education consistently and confidently. A parallel online learning platform extends reach beyond the classroom, while targeted social media awareness campaigns engage parents, religious leaders, and community figures as reinforcing partners in the child's social development.

Blu Foundation operates on the conviction that manners and etiquette are not peripheral to a child's education — they are foundational. Evidence from comparable NGO-led programmes demonstrates that structured, values-based interventions in early childhood settings produce measurable improvements in social behaviour, peer relationships, and academic outcomes [SOURCE_BD_03]. Bangladesh has the institutional infrastructure, the motivated teaching workforce, and the cultural traditions of courtesy and respect — what is missing is the dedicated curriculum and the organisational architecture to deliver it at scale. Blu Foundation exists to build exactly that.

## 4. Core Values

Blu Foundation is guided by five foundational values that shape every programme decision, partnership, and communication. These values are not aspirational decoration — they are operationalised into curriculum content, staff appraisal frameworks, and community engagement standards. Research confirms that organisations and schools that make their character values explicit and behaviorally defined produce measurable, lasting outcomes for children [SOURCE_UK_01].

### 4.1 Respect | সম্মান [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Respect is the cornerstone of all human interaction and the primary disposition Blu Foundation seeks to cultivate in children and communities. We teach respect as an active practice: acknowledging the inherent worth of every person regardless of age, gender, faith, or background. In our programmes, respect is demonstrated through language, posture, listening, and the ways children address teachers, parents, peers, and strangers.

### 4.2 Kindness | দয়া [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Kindness is the willingness to consider the feelings and needs of others and to act in ways that ease rather than increase suffering. Blu Foundation grounds its understanding of kindness in both universal ethical principles and Islamic teachings on compassion (রহমত [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]) that resonate deeply within Bangladeshi culture. We teach children that small acts — sharing, greeting warmly, offering help — constitute the daily architecture of a kind society.

### 4.3 Integrity | সততা [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Integrity is the alignment between inner values and outward behaviour — doing what is right even when unobserved. For primary-age children, integrity is introduced through concepts of honesty, promise-keeping, and taking responsibility for one's actions. Blu Foundation's curriculum uses age-appropriate stories and scenarios drawn from Bangladeshi everyday life to make integrity tangible and practisable rather than abstract.

### 4.4 Discipline | শৃঙ্খলা [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Discipline, in Blu Foundation's framing, is not punishment — it is the capacity to regulate one's own impulses, follow through on commitments, and act with order and purpose. We teach self-discipline as a skill that can be learned and strengthened, not a trait one either possesses or lacks. Children who internalise positive self-discipline are better equipped to learn, to cooperate, and to resist the peer pressures that lead to harmful behaviour.

### 4.5 Community | সম্প্রদায় [NEEDS BENGALI TRANSLATION]

Community reflects our belief that character is not formed in isolation — it is shaped by, and in turn shapes, the social fabric around us. Blu Foundation engages parents, religious leaders, teachers, and neighbours as active participants in children's moral development. A child who sees respectful behaviour modelled consistently across home, school, and mosque or temple internalises those norms far more durably than one who receives lessons only within the classroom.

## 5. The Problem We Are Solving

Bangladesh is a nation of enormous warmth, hospitality, and social tradition. Yet a growing body of evidence — from national surveys, UNICEF reports, and peer-reviewed academic research — documents a measurable and alarming decline in the culture of respectful behaviour among young people, with consequences visible in classrooms, communities, and the justice system alike.

The scale of peer-on-peer and adult-on-child mistreatment within Bangladeshi schools is striking. A structured survey of 556 students found that nearly 50% had experienced bullying as victims, and a staggering 78.5% reported that teachers themselves engaged in bullying behaviour [SOURCE_BD_09]. UNICEF data corroborates the breadth of the problem: 35% of students aged 13–15 reported being bullied within the last 30 days [SOURCE_BD_10]. Physical disciplinary practices against children — including violent punishment — show a lifetime prevalence of 99% in Bangladesh [SOURCE_BD_11]. These are not marginal statistics. They describe an environment in which the very adults charged with modelling dignified behaviour are frequently its violators, and in which children have no structured framework for understanding what respectful treatment looks, sounds, and feels like.

The curriculum is failing to address this. Bangladesh's national competency-based curriculum framework does include a Values (V) component — on paper. In practice, this component is not delivered in actual classrooms [SOURCE_BD_07]. Moral education, where it exists, is channelled exclusively through religion textbooks; no standalone social etiquette curriculum exists anywhere in the national system [SOURCE_BD_05]. Researchers studying Bangladeshi schools have observed that ethical values are absent from classroom instruction and that younger generations show a measurable decline in moral values compared with their predecessors [SOURCE_BD_01]. The system acknowledges the importance of character — and then provides no mechanism for building it.

The consequences of this vacuum are well-documented. Moral degradation and social deviance have been directly linked to juvenile delinquency in Bangladesh, with researchers identifying the absence of structured values education as a root cause rather than a contributing factor [SOURCE_BD_12]. The developmental window is also closing: research consistently shows that early childhood is the optimal period for character formation, and that norms absorbed in the primary years become the scaffolding on which adolescent and adult behaviour is built [SOURCE_BD_03]. Globalisation compounds the urgency — as digital media and external cultural influences accelerate, the traditional community and family structures that once transmitted etiquette informally are weakening, and no formal substitute has been put in their place [SOURCE_BD_03].

The evidence also points clearly toward the most effective delivery mechanism. NGO-led pilot programmes in Bangladesh have demonstrated consistently higher impact on both cognitive and social outcomes than equivalent government-run programmes [SOURCE_BD_25]. NGOs have been specifically identified in the literature as key actors in Bangladesh's social development landscape, particularly for filling gaps that the public system cannot or does not address [SOURCE_BD_03]. Blu Foundation is designed precisely for this role: it is nimble enough to develop evidence-based content rapidly, trusted enough to work within government school structures, and mission-focused enough to sustain the long-term commitment that character education requires.

## 6. Theory of Change (Summary)

> *Full Theory of Change detailed in BLU_D03_Theory_of_Change.md*

Blu Foundation's theory of change rests on a simple but evidence-backed premise: when primary school children receive structured, culturally grounded, teacher-delivered instruction in manners and etiquette — and when that instruction is reinforced at home and in the community — measurable, lasting improvements in respectful behaviour follow at the individual, school, and community level.

| **Inputs** | **Activities** | **Outputs** | **Short-Term Outcomes** | **Long-Term Impact** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed research base; curriculum development expertise; teacher trainers; funding | Develop manners & etiquette textbooks (Classes 1–5) | Published, curriculum-aligned textbooks in Bengali | Children demonstrate knowledge of core etiquette norms | A school culture characterised by mutual respect and reduced bullying |
| Teacher training curriculum; facilitators; school partnerships | Deliver teacher training workshops (in-person and online) | Trained teachers with certification; refresher modules | Teachers model and reinforce respectful behaviour consistently | National adoption of manners education as a recognised discipline |
| Digital platform; content team | Launch online courses for students, teachers, and parents | Accessible e-learning modules; enrolled users | Parents and community engage actively with values education | Community-wide norms shift; measurable decline in social deviance |
| Social media strategy; communications staff | Run awareness campaigns targeting parents and communities | Impressions; engagement; community discussions | Reduced tolerance for bullying and disrespectful behaviour in homes and public spaces | Policy change: government integration of etiquette curriculum into national framework |
| M&E framework; data collection tools | Monitor programme delivery; measure behaviour change | Quarterly reports; annual impact evaluations | Validated evidence of programme effectiveness | Replicable model adopted across South Asia |

The logic connecting these elements is sequential and cumulative. Blu Foundation first ensures quality content exists — textbooks and training materials grounded in peer-reviewed research and adapted for Bangladeshi culture and Islam. Quality content delivered through trained teachers produces children who not only know the norms of respectful behaviour but practise them habitually. Habitual practice within school walls creates a changed social environment that reinforces the same norms, making respectful behaviour the path of least resistance rather than the exception.

The second link in the chain is community alignment. Research is clear that in-school interventions produce significantly stronger outcomes when parents and community figures are engaged as co-educators rather than passive observers [SOURCE_BD_03]. Blu Foundation's communications and digital programme is designed to ensure that the values a child encounters in the classroom are visible, named, and celebrated at home. When a parent understands what the school is teaching — and why — they become an active multiplier of the programme's reach.

The ultimate aspiration of Blu Foundation's theory of change is systemic: for the Bangladeshi government to recognise values and etiquette education as a substantive, deliverable curriculum component and to integrate Blu Foundation's model into the national framework. The evidence base for NGO-led pilots producing policy change in Bangladesh's education sector is well-established [SOURCE_BD_25]. Blu Foundation builds with this trajectory in view from the outset.

## 7. Target Beneficiaries

Blu Foundation's programmes are designed for three concentric groups. Each group is essential; the model loses coherence if any one group is neglected.

### 7.1 Primary Beneficiaries — Class 1–5 Students (Ages 6–11)

The primary beneficiaries are children enrolled in Classes 1 through 5 across Bangladesh's government primary schools, Registered Non-Government Primary Schools (RNGPS), and private primary institutions. These children, aged approximately 6 to 11 years, occupy the developmental window that research identifies as optimal for character formation — old enough to reason about social norms, young enough for those norms to become automatic and internalised [SOURCE_BD_03].

Within this group, Blu Foundation prioritises children in under-resourced government and RNGPS schools in urban areas during Phase 1, expanding to rural and semi-urban contexts in later phases. These children are disproportionately exposed to the bullying and disciplinary violence documented in the research literature [SOURCE_BD_09], and have the least access to supplementary character education outside the school system.

### 7.2 Secondary Beneficiaries — Primary School Teachers (Approx. 400,000 Nationally)

Bangladesh's approximately 400,000 primary school teachers are both a delivery vehicle and a beneficiary group in their own right. Research documents that teacher bullying is itself a significant problem [SOURCE_BD_09]; addressing this requires equipping teachers not only with a new curriculum but with a changed understanding of their own role in modelling respectful behaviour. Blu Foundation's teacher training programme treats teachers as professionals deserving high-quality development — not as passive conduits for a packaged curriculum.

Trained teachers become permanent assets within their schools and communities. A teacher who has internalised the principles of respectful pedagogy and implemented them for one cohort of children continues to deliver that value for every subsequent cohort they teach. This multiplier effect is central to Blu Foundation's sustainability model.

### 7.3 Tertiary Beneficiaries — Parents, Community Leaders, and Religious Figures

The tertiary group comprises the adults who shape children's social environment outside the school: parents and guardians, local community leaders (union parishad members, school management committee members), and religious figures (imams, madrasa teachers). This group does not receive formal curriculum-based programming; instead, they are reached through Blu Foundation's awareness campaigns, community events, and online learning content designed for adult audiences.

Their role is reinforcement and legitimacy. When an imam delivers a Friday sermon that aligns with the values Blu Foundation is teaching in classrooms — respect, kindness, integrity — the child experiences cultural coherence rather than compartmentalisation. When parents understand the "why" behind the programme, they ask about it at home and reinforce it naturally. Blu Foundation actively cultivates these relationships as a strategic priority, not an afterthought.

## 8. Geographic Scope

Blu Foundation's expansion follows a phased model designed to generate strong evidence in controlled contexts before scaling. Each phase builds on validated learning from the previous phase.

### Phase 1 — Year 1: Urban Pilot
> [FOUNDER INPUT REQUIRED: Select pilot district — recommended: Dhaka metropolitan area or Chattogram]

Blu Foundation will select a single urban pilot district to launch its first textbook, teacher training cohort, and community awareness campaign. An urban starting point is deliberate: access to schools is more consistent, teacher populations are larger and more accessible for training, and media reach for the awareness campaign is stronger. The pilot will target a minimum of 10 schools and 50 teachers, with baseline and endline measurement of student behaviour and teacher practice.

### Phase 2 — Year 2: Regional Expansion

Building on validated pilot results, Blu Foundation will expand to 3 districts across 2 divisions. This phase introduces geographic diversity — including at least one semi-urban or peri-urban district — to test the model's adaptability and begin reaching children outside metropolitan areas. Teacher training will be adapted for delivery through regional education offices.

### Phase 3 — Year 3: National Presence

In Year 3, Blu Foundation will establish programme presence across all 8 divisions of Bangladesh, reaching both urban and rural schools. This phase will coincide with advocacy efforts targeting the National Curriculum and Textbook Board (NCTB) and the Directorate of Primary Education (DPE) to explore integration of Blu Foundation's etiquette curriculum into the national framework.

## 9. Legal Structure

Blu Foundation is in the process of selecting its formal legal structure. Three options exist within Bangladeshi law, each with distinct implications for funding sources, governance obligations, and operational scope.

### Option A — Voluntary Social Welfare Organisation (Department of Social Services)

Registration under the Voluntary Social Welfare Agencies (Registration and Control) Ordinance 1961 through the Department of Social Services (DSS) is the most accessible pathway for a domestically focused NGO. It is suitable for organisations whose funding will come entirely from Bangladeshi sources — individual donors, local corporate sponsors, and government grants. DSS registration does not authorise the receipt of foreign donations, which limits organisational growth potential significantly for an NGO with international ambitions.

### Option B — NGO Affairs Bureau (NGOAB) Registration

Registration with the NGO Affairs Bureau (NGOAB) under the Prime Minister's Office is required for any organisation that receives foreign donations. NGOAB registration imposes more rigorous reporting obligations — including project-by-project approval for foreign-funded activities — but grants substantially expanded operational legitimacy and access to international donor networks. Most established Bangladeshi NGOs operating at scale (BRAC, Prothom Alo Trust, Friendship) hold NGOAB registration.

### Option C — Company Limited by Guarantee (Companies Act 1994)

Incorporation as a company limited by guarantee under the Companies Act 1994 provides Blu Foundation with full legal personhood — the ability to enter contracts, own property, and pursue litigation in its own name. This structure is the most administratively complex and costly to establish and maintain, but offers the strongest legal protections for organisational assets and the most credible corporate governance profile for partnerships with international institutions and private sector actors.

### Recommended Structure

**Blu Foundation recommends Option B (NGOAB registration) as the primary legal framework, supplemented by DSS registration for domestic operations.** If any international funding is anticipated — from bilateral donors, international foundations, or diaspora sources — NGOAB registration is not optional; it is legally required. NGOAB registration also signals credibility to government partners at the Ministry of Primary and Mass Education (MoPME) and the Directorate of Primary Education (DPE), both of which are critical institutional relationships for Blu Foundation's curriculum integration goals.

## 10. Organisational Structure

Blu Foundation is designed with a lean, mission-aligned structure appropriate for an early-stage NGO, with clear lines of accountability and defined growth pathways for each function.

```
Board of Directors (7–9 members)
  └── Executive Director
        ├── Program Director
        │     ├── Curriculum Lead
        │     ├── Teacher Training Coordinator
        │     └── M&E Officer
        ├── Finance & Administration Officer
        └── Communications & Digital Officer
```

### Role Descriptions

**Board of Directors**
The Board of Directors holds ultimate fiduciary and strategic responsibility for Blu Foundation. It approves annual budgets, reviews programme strategy, ensures legal compliance, and appoints and evaluates the Executive Director. The Board meets at least quarterly and exercises its authority through formal resolution rather than day-to-day operational involvement.

**Executive Director**
The Executive Director (ED) is the most senior operational leader of Blu Foundation, responsible for implementing the Board's strategic direction, managing all staff, representing the organisation externally, and ensuring financial accountability. The ED is the primary relationship holder with government partners, major donors, and peer organisations, and reports directly to the Board of Directors.

**Program Director**
The Program Director oversees the design, delivery, and quality of all Blu Foundation's substantive programmes — textbook development, teacher training, and online learning. This role requires deep familiarity with Bangladesh's primary education system and strong project management capability. The Program Director supervises the Curriculum Lead, Teacher Training Coordinator, and M&E Officer.

**Curriculum Lead**
The Curriculum Lead is responsible for the research, writing, review, and revision of all Blu Foundation curriculum materials, including student textbooks and teacher guides. This role works closely with external academic reviewers and cultural consultants to ensure all content is pedagogically sound, culturally appropriate, and aligned with national curriculum standards.

**Teacher Training Coordinator**
The Teacher Training Coordinator designs and delivers Blu Foundation's teacher professional development programmes, manages relationships with pilot schools and regional education offices, and ensures that trained teachers receive adequate ongoing support. This role requires experience in adult learning and familiarity with Bangladesh's primary teacher workforce.

**M&E Officer**
The Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (M&E) Officer designs and implements Blu Foundation's data collection and measurement systems, produces quarterly progress reports and annual impact evaluations, and translates evidence into actionable programme improvements. This role is essential for maintaining donor accountability and building the evidence base that will support Blu Foundation's advocacy objectives.

**Finance & Administration Officer**
The Finance & Administration Officer manages all financial reporting, procurement, payroll, and compliance obligations — including NGOAB annual reporting and audit requirements. This role ensures that Blu Foundation's internal controls meet the standards required by both domestic regulators and international donors.

**Communications & Digital Officer**
The Communications & Digital Officer manages Blu Foundation's external presence: social media campaigns, website, online learning platform, press relationships, and community outreach events. This role translates programme impact into public-facing narratives that build awareness, trust, and financial support for the organisation's work.

## 11. Board of Directors

Blu Foundation's Board of Directors should comprise 7–9 members, with a majority being independent directors who have no financial or familial relationship with the founding team. This structure protects organisational integrity and demonstrates governance credibility to donors and government partners alike.

### Recommended Composition

The Board should include, at minimum:
- **At least 2 women**, reflecting Blu Foundation's commitment to gender equity in leadership and the demonstrated importance of diverse perspectives in educational programme design
- **One academic or researcher** with expertise in education, child development, or social science, to ensure programme decisions are grounded in evidence
- **One legal professional** familiar with NGO law in Bangladesh, to advise on NGOAB compliance, contracts, and governance obligations
- **One finance professional** (chartered accountant or equivalent) to chair the Finance and Audit Committee and oversee organisational financial health
- **One educator** with direct experience in Bangladesh's primary school system, to ensure programme design remains connected to classroom realities

### Governance Cadence

The full Board meets at minimum once per quarter (four meetings annually). The Executive Director prepares a written report for each meeting covering programme progress, financial performance, and strategic risks. Between meetings, a smaller Executive Committee (comprising the Board Chair, Treasurer, and one elected member) has authority to make time-sensitive decisions within defined parameters.

## 12. Advisory Council

Blu Foundation recommends establishing a 5–7 person Advisory Council to complement the Board's governance role with specialised technical and cultural expertise. Advisory Council members do not hold fiduciary responsibility but provide strategic guidance, open institutional doors, and lend credibility to the organisation's public profile.

### Recommended Advisory Council Composition

- **Bangladesh Education Researcher (BRAC University Institute of Educational Development or equivalent):** Provides ongoing connection to the academic literature on Bangladeshi child development and school quality, and facilitates research partnerships.
- **Islamic Scholar (recognised madrasa or Islamic institution):** Provides cultural and theological endorsement of Blu Foundation's values framework, ensuring that curriculum content is credible and accepted within Muslim-majority communities. This role is particularly important for community outreach in rural and peri-urban areas.
- **Child Development Psychologist:** Ensures that programme content and pedagogy are developmentally appropriate for children aged 6–11, and advises on behaviour change mechanisms underpinning the curriculum.
- **Corporate CSR Representative:** Provides connection to private sector funding, business community credibility, and practical insight into how Blu Foundation can design employer-relevant social outcomes.
- **Retired Government Education Official (Ministry of Primary and Mass Education or Directorate of Primary Education):** Provides insider knowledge of government education processes, facilitates introductions to current officials, and advises on the realistic pathway to national curriculum integration.

Advisory Council members serve two-year renewable terms and are expected to attend two meetings per year plus be available for occasional consultation.

## 13. Research Evidence Base

The need for Blu Foundation's work is not a matter of perception or cultural nostalgia — it is documented by a convergent body of peer-reviewed research, government data, and international organisation reports. Multiple independent sources confirm both the severity of the problem and the effectiveness of the NGO-led solution Blu Foundation proposes.

Bangladesh's academic literature paints a consistent picture of institutional failure on values education. Researchers have documented that ethical values are absent in Bangladeshi schools, that younger generations demonstrate measurable moral decline compared to their predecessors, and that the national curriculum's nominal inclusion of values content is not translated into classroom practice [SOURCE_BD_01], [SOURCE_BD_07]. Moral education exists only as a sub-component of religious instruction [SOURCE_BD_05], leaving children who attend secular government schools with no structured exposure to social etiquette, empathy, or conflict resolution skills whatsoever. Meanwhile, the window for intervention is closing: developmental science identifies the primary school years as the critical period for character formation, and researchers studying Bangladesh specifically have called on NGOs to step into this space [SOURCE_BD_03].

The consequences of inaction are measured in human suffering. Bullying rates in Bangladeshi schools are among the highest documented in South Asia [SOURCE_BD_09], [SOURCE_BD_10], and the link between moral disengagement in childhood and juvenile delinquency in adolescence is empirically established in the Bangladeshi context [SOURCE_BD_12]. Physical violence against children — normalised through intergenerational transmission — shows a near-universal lifetime prevalence of 99% [SOURCE_BD_11], demonstrating that the problem cannot be addressed through curriculum content alone but requires the concurrent transformation of teacher practice and community norms that Blu Foundation's model is designed to achieve. NGO-led programmes in Bangladesh have demonstrated superior outcomes compared to government-run equivalents [SOURCE_BD_25], making the case for Blu Foundation's model not merely plausible but evidence-backed.
